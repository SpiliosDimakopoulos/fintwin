\section{Related models and hypotheses}\label{sec:framework}

\subsection{What the simulator is}
\twin{} is a stock-and-flow model of a firm's financial system whose parameters are re-estimated automatically from each new filing. In the taxonomy of \citet{kritzinger2018} it is a \emph{digital shadow} with a human in the loop: it is synchronized from the firm's filings, but no decision taken in the model is executed in the firm. Its synchronization frequency is bounded by the quarterly reporting cycle, and its state can be rewound to any past filing date, which is what makes a point-in-time backtest possible. It supports what-if analysis through scenarios, management levers and reverse stress tests. The evidence in this paper concerns the model's forecasts, not the label.

\subsection{Relation to existing models}
Table~\ref{tab:framework} places the simulator among four families of models that address related questions.

\emph{Accounting-based distress scores} \citep{beaver1966,altman1968,ohlson1980,zmijewski1984} and their hazard-model successors \citep{shumway2001,chava2004,campbell2008} map ratios to the probability of an event. They are estimated on default samples and are not conditional on a scenario. \emph{Structural credit models} \citep{merton1974,leland1994} derive default probabilities from asset value and volatility, inferred from equity prices. Their state variable is a single asset value, not cash, working capital or a maturity ladder, so they cannot say whether a given revenue path exhausts liquidity before the debt is due. \emph{Corporate financial-planning models} \citep{carleton1970,warren1971} are simultaneous-equation projections of the financial statements under managerial assumptions. They are close in spirit to the simulator's accounting core but are deterministic, specified by the analyst and rarely validated out of sample. \emph{Top-down corporate stress tests} run by central banks, the IMF and the OECD \citep{imf2020,roulet2020} apply macroeconomic and sector-level scenarios to firm-level data, without firm-specific feedback between financing conditions and operations. Machine-learning classifiers \citep{barboza2017,mai2019,jones2017} raise the accuracy of distress prediction but, like the scores, are not conditional on a scenario. Finally, the accounting literature on \emph{cash-flow prediction} \citep{dechow1998,barth2001,kim2005,lev2010,nallareddy2020}, on cross-sectional forecasts of earnings \citep{hou2012,li2014} and on the mean reversion of profitability \citep{fama2000} provides the simplest rivals for the simulator's transmission mechanics: rules that map revenue into cash through margins, accruals and working capital.

Simulation models of firms appear in practitioner and early-stage work \citep{westphal2024,anshari2022,bhatia2026,lyytinen2024}, none of which tests a firm-level model against simple rivals out of sample. The simulator combines elements that these families keep apart: an accounting identity that holds on every path, firm-specific parameters estimated from filings, contractual debt maturities and credit lines, path-dependent financing constraints (feedback loops), and a probabilistic description of uncertainty. Whether this combination forecasts anything that the simpler members of each family do not is an empirical question.

\subsection{Forecast evaluation}
We score point forecasts with absolute and squared loss and compare them with Diebold--Mariano tests \citep{diebold1995}, test forecast encompassing \citep{chong1986,fair1990,harvey1998,clark2001} with standard errors clustered by firm and year \citep{petersen2009,cameron2008}, and control the error rate of a pre-specified family of comparisons with the stepwise procedure of \citet{romano2005}, the model confidence set of \citet{hansen2011} and the test for superior predictive ability of \citet{hansen2005}. Combinations of forecasts are evaluated against the evidence that simple combinations are hard to beat \citep{bates1969,clemen1989,timmermann2006,claeskens2016}. Predictive distributions are scored with the continuous ranked probability score and probability integral transform histograms \citep{diebold1998,gneiting2007,gneiting2007b}, and event probabilities with the Brier score and its decomposition into reliability and resolution \citep{murphy1973}.

\subsection{Hypotheses}
The evaluation has two pillars. The first asks whether the simulator's \emph{point} forecast of cash flow, given the revenue that followed, contains information beyond persistence. The second asks whether its \emph{distribution} of outcomes and its liquidity-stress probabilities are better than simple statistical rivals.
\begin{description}
\item[H2 (transmission, pillar A).] Given the realized revenue path, the simulator's cash-flow forecast contains information that is not contained in margin rules (including long-window and industry-shrunk margins) or in a per-firm regression of cash flow on revenue and its change.
\item[Density (pillar B).] The simulator's predictive distribution of eight-quarter cash flow, given the realized revenue path, has a lower CRPS than a Gaussian and an empirical distribution of residuals around the 16-quarter margin.
\item[Shortfall (pillar B).] The simulator's probability that cash plus undrawn credit falls below zero is a better forecast of realized liquidity shortfalls, in Brier score, than a logit on opening ratios.
\end{description}
\noindent Two further confirmatory tests concern the corrections themselves (Section~\ref{sec:design}): that the corrected specification has a smaller median error than the earlier one (S1), and that its response of cash flow to revenue is between 0.8 and 1.25 times the realized one. H2 is the transmission hypothesis of an earlier evaluation of this model \selfcitep, kept with its decision rule unchanged; the ranking (H1) and feedback-loop (H3) hypotheses of that evaluation, whose rules were written after their first results had been seen, are reported as descriptive evidence in Section~\ref{sec:secondary}. The plan labels the density and shortfall tests B1 and B2; we use names to avoid a clash with the loops B1 and B2.
